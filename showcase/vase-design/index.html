Product Design

From a Child's Drawing to a 3D Printed Vase

A family design project that turns a hand-drawn vase into a 3D concept, then into a first printed prototype.

Date
June 2026
Status
First Prototype
Built With
Hand sketch, Gemini prompt, Meshy 3D, 3D printing, Family design

This vase began with something very ordinary and very precious: a drawing my daughter made at school. She drew a vase, and when I first saw it, I felt there was something special in it. It was not only a picture of a container. It was a small object full of her interests, memories, and imagination.

So we decided to try a family design experiment: could we take this drawing from paper, develop it into a digital 3D concept, and eventually make a physical sample that could sit on a desk or shelf?

Reading the Story Inside the Drawing

The most interesting part of the drawing is that the decoration is not random. Each area of the vase carries a piece of her life.

The two handles are designed like piano keys because she plays the piano. Handles are usually just functional parts of a vase, but here they become part of the story. Their curved form still helps the vase keep the feeling of a classical amphora, while the black-and-white key pattern connects the object to music and practice.

The mouth of the vase is inspired by books and bookshelves. This part represents learning, reading, and the steady accumulation of knowledge. The middle section is about art. She enjoys drawing and is currently learning oil painting and sketching, so this area includes a rose, a paintbrush, and freehand marks. It feels like the center of the vase has become a small canvas.

The lower section is about badminton. Because she likes playing badminton, we imagined shuttlecocks and rackets moving around the base, as if the game is happening in motion. The upper half of the vase feels quiet, with music, reading, and painting. The lower half feels more energetic, with shuttlecocks flying back and forth.

There is also a tower in the design. This came from a family trip to Guangzhou, where we saw the Canton Tower. That small detail makes the vase more than a collection of hobbies. It also records a real family memory.

Turning the Idea Into a Prompt

Before moving from hand drawing to digital design, the most important step was not opening a 3D tool. It was translating the visual idea into design language.

We needed to ask a few questions:

  • What is the overall shape of the vase?
  • Which symbols must be preserved?
  • Which elements are inspiration only and can be rearranged?
  • Should the final object feel like a toy, a decorative sculpture, or a practical vase?
  • If it will be 3D printed, which details might be too thin, too small, or too fragile?

I used Gemini to help organise the idea into an English prompt, then used Meshy to generate a 3D model. The original prompt was:

A three-dimensional round vase, shaped like an ancient Greek amphora,
features a matte black ceramic body adorned with intricate white relief
or etched patterns.

The ring-shaped handle is decorated with abstract black-and-white
geometric designs and piano-key textures, while the middle section is
adorned with an elegant rose, a few freehand strokes, and a paintbrush.

The base features a silhouette-like city skyline in black, within which
the Auckland Sky Tower is visible, along with the outlines of several
badminton shuttlecocks and rackets.

Under soft lighting, the vase reveals a striking contrast between its
sharp contours and intricate textures.

This prompt helped turn “a child’s drawing” into “a product description that a 3D tool can understand.” It included the amphora-like shape, black-and-white ceramic contrast, piano-key handles, painting elements, city tower, and badminton motifs.

It also revealed a useful point for revision: the prompt says “Auckland Sky Tower”, but the true inspiration was the Canton Tower in Guangzhou. That difference is a good reminder that AI generation is not the same as final design. It gives us a fast draft, but the designer still needs to check the meaning, correct the details, and guide the next version.

A Simple Product Design Workflow

If we treat this as a product design exercise, the process can be understood in several stages.

The first stage is inspiration. Inspiration can come from a drawing, a trip, a hobby, a memory, or a real-life problem. At this stage, the goal is not to judge the idea too quickly. The goal is to collect what feels meaningful. For a child, this stage is especially valuable because it shows that personal interests can become material for design.

The second stage is concept organisation. We grouped the drawing into themes: music, reading, painting, badminton, and travel memory. Then we considered where each theme should live on the vase. Piano keys fit naturally on the handles because handles are long curved forms. Books fit near the mouth because that area already has edges and layers. Badminton elements fit near the base because the lower band can show movement around the object.

The third stage is the hand sketch. A sketch does not need to be perfect. Its job is to capture the idea quickly. Many products begin as rough drawings, notes, wireframes, or collages. A sketch makes it easier to discuss questions such as: Which elements should stay? Is the shape recognisable? Can someone else understand the story?

The fourth stage is digital design. At this point, the idea needs to become more specific. Proportion, material, colour, thickness, relief depth, and support structure all start to matter. AI tools and 3D tools can help speed up this stage by turning words and sketches into a model that can be rotated and inspected. But the human designer still has to decide whether the model matches the original intention.

The fifth stage is sample making. A first 3D print is usually not the final product. It is a physical test. We can check the size, structure, surface detail, balance, and whether the patterns are visible enough after printing. A model that looks beautiful on screen may still need changes before it works as a real object.

The sixth stage is revision. Most products go through several versions before they feel finished. Small textures may need to be simplified. Thin parts may need to be thickened. Symbols may need to be moved so the story reads more clearly. Material and colour choices may change as well. Each sample gives feedback for the next version.

The final stage is defining the finished product. A product is not finished simply because it has been printed. It is finished when the design purpose is clear, the visual language is stable, the making method can be repeated, and the ownership and display notes are properly recorded. For this vase, the first prototype proves that the idea can move from paper to object. The next step is to improve the proportions, pattern clarity, and surface treatment.

What Could Improve in the Next Version?

The next version could focus on several areas.

First, we can improve symbol recognition. When someone sees the handles, can they immediately connect them with piano keys? When they see the top, can they recognise books and shelves? Second, we can improve the visual hierarchy. The painting elements, badminton elements, and tower should not compete with each other. They should form clear zones that guide the viewer around the object.

Third, we can test printability. If a relief pattern is too shallow, it may disappear after printing or finishing. If a line is too thin, it may break or become unclear. The design may need stronger edges, fewer tiny details, or deeper engraving.

Colour is another direction to explore. Black and white work well for piano keys, sketching, and relief patterns. But a future version could test matte black, warm white, small gold accents, or a more ceramic-like surface finish. The goal would be to keep the personal symbols visible while making the object feel more refined.

It may also be helpful to create a labelled design map. This could divide the vase into mouth, handles, middle body, and base, then explain the meaning of each section. With that map, viewers would not only see an attractive object. They would also understand the family memory and learning process behind it.
